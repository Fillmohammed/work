# Seth Godin modules 2–6: digest and what each one means for Project 1%

Source: the five transcripts uploaded on 10 Oct 2026 (Module 2 to Module 6), read line by line. Module 1 was not
in the upload. Quotes are verbatim from the transcripts. The "So for Project 1%" lines are my application, not
Seth's words.

---

## Module 2: Brand advertising vs direct response advertising

**What he says**

- Direct marketing is "action marketing… measured marketing". If $1 of ads returns $2, keep going "until you make
  all the dollars that it's possible to make, and then you should stop."
- Direct response doesn't scale forever: "if you run a coupon too many times, if you interrupt too many people…
  it stops working."
- Meta's algorithm hooks advertisers: "when you start buying these ads, your response rate will be great. And then
  inevitably, over time, it will get worse and worse and worse."
- The real cost of that addiction: advertisers "stop paying attention to what matters, which is finding the tribe,
  earning the attention of the tribe, building conversations within and among the tribe… as opposed to simply
  strip mining their attention."
- Permission (Seth's own invention, email marketing): "anticipated, personal, and relevant messages… the money you
  spend to earn permission always pays for itself."
- The strategist's job: "ask why? Why am I even spending the time or the money? Am I measuring the right thing?"

**So for Project 1%**

- Today the whole launch is cold, interruptive Meta traffic to a long sales video. That's the part Seth says decays.
- Richard already holds permission with thousands of people (3,000+ property clients, a 5.9k-subscriber YouTube
  channel, Skool, email lists). The first tribe should come from there, not from strangers.
- "Measuring the right thing": a $9 sale is not the goal. Men finishing 35 days, staying in the brotherhood and
  bringing a friend are.

## Module 3: Creating culture and stories worth sharing

**What he says**

- "A brand is not a logo… A brand is a promise." Nike could open a hotel and we'd know what it's like. Hyatt
  shoes? No idea. "Nike has a brand, but Hyatt just has a logo."
- A brand earns "the benefit of the doubt" (people rated Bing results higher when the Google logo was on top).
- Brand loyalty has two tests: "if your thing costs more than your competitors, does anyone buy yours?" and "if
  you announce something, are people interested in it?"
- Pick by psychographics, not demographics: "What do people believe before we even get there? What do people want
  before we get there?… where are we finding our pain and our fear and our dreams?"
- The three things people want: **status** ("who's up, who's down"), **affiliation** ("who's like me"), and
  **freedom from fear**.
- TOMS: an $80 logo'd espadrille, sold to "almost no one", which "is exactly the right thing to do". When a friend
  said "nice shoes, where'd you get them?", the early adopter got status, and the friend got tension. It spread
  person to person. TOMS coffee failed: "no one who's drinking a cup of coffee says to you, 'Oh, interesting
  coffee, where'd you get it?'" No conversation, no tribe.
- The difference that matters: "Here's the social media ad, please buy some coffee" versus "I'm going to help this
  group of people get to where they want to go."

**So for Project 1%**

- Richard's brand promise, as he states it himself, is honesty: "the most honest one you'll hear from this year."
  Everything in the funnel either keeps or breaks that promise. See `02-positioning-and-offer.md` for the audit.
- A $9 course has no "nice shoes" moment. Nobody asks "where'd you get that $9 course?" The program needs a
  visible, talkable thing (a cohort, a code, a coin, a brotherhood a man is *proud* to be in).
- The want is status and affiliation for men who have quietly lost both (no close friends, a marriage that's gone
  quiet, a body they avoid in the mirror). Freedom from fear: fear of the slipped disc, the divorce, becoming their
  dad.

## Module 4: The smallest viable audience

**What he says**

- The agency argued whether the kid in the ad should be a boy or a girl. Seth suggested someone in between: wrong,
  because the opportunity "is to find not the biggest possible audience, but the smallest viable audience."
- "How few people would you need to delight or thrill?… for your project to work?" Picking that number "puts you on
  the hook."
- The Apple Newton sold 100,000 units in month one and still failed, because buyers "weren't a group. They were
  just a bunch… They weren't responding to the same story."
- Purple Cow: Seth printed 10,000 copies in a milk carton and offered them to people who already trusted him (his
  Fast Company readers) for $5 postage. He also sold 12-packs. A 12-pack forces you to give copies away, and giving
  them away makes you look like "this generous leader". The milk carton on a desk "sent a message to your boss…
  let's talk about this." It went on to sell over a million copies.
- "It always begins… not with the biggest possible audience, but with the smallest viable audience, the one you
  can live with."

**So for Project 1%**

- The ads, the sales video and the page speak to three different men (rich and empty at 50; "24 or 54, broke or
  doing well"; men "chasing more"). That's a bunch, not a group. Pick one.
- Start where trust already exists (his existing audience), the way Seth started with his readers.
- Build a 12-pack equivalent: a "bring a brother" mechanic, so joining makes the member look like a leader to
  his friends.

## Module 5: Building an authentic voice

**What he says**

- "Authenticity is a crock, it's a trap… authenticity is for friends and family… professionals don't bring
  authenticity. They bring consistency."
- "Your brand is a promise. Are you gonna keep it or not?… make a promise and keep it."
- Agencies break the promise when they're rewarded for awards or clicks: "they show up with one offs… ads that
  don't match the promise of the brand… gimmicks."
- "A purple cow isn't a gimmick. A purple cow is a service… this is who we are and what we believe in. This is the
  change we're here to make. Do you want to come on this journey or not?"
- Platforms reward going off script and picking fights "'cause that makes the social media companies more money.
  Not 'cause it makes you succeed."
- Good decisions are not good outcomes (Annie Duke). A good creative decision: (1) don't run out of time or money,
  (2) be strategically coherent: "the change we seek to make and who's it for and what's it for." If it then fails,
  "we've learned one thing that doesn't work."

**So for Project 1%**

- This sharpens your point. The goal isn't for Richard to "be more authentic". It's for every touchpoint to keep
  one promise, consistently: ad, page, video, checkout, onboarding, lessons, community, follow-up emails, the call.
- The "simp / cynic, don't click" lines and the "it might make you angry" opener are the platform-rewarded fights
  Seth warns about. They don't serve the promise.
- Run tests as decisions, not bets: a coherent hypothesis, a budget that can't run out, and a clear lesson either
  way.

## Module 6: Ad positioning

**What he says**

- "Positioning is a generous act." Draw an XY grid, find an under-inhabited corner, but choose axes "where either
  axis is a good thing."
- Volvo vs Ferrari: each dealer should happily send the wrong customer to the other. "What it means to have a
  position is that you can eagerly and honestly send people to what others think of as your competition."
- "There isn't a best position, there's simply a useful position."
- Supreme's position isn't "you need a t-shirt". It's "for people who view clothing as a sign of status and a
  certain kind of affiliation… please, don't come in here and tell us your stuff's too expensive, it's not for
  you."
- "If you're selling life insurance and your slogan is, our life insurance is just like their life insurance, you
  don't have a position."
- "Stand for something and not decide to stand for everything."
- Strategy cards:
  - **Don't play games you can't win.** "You can't out Amazon Amazon… go find one of those things and claim it."
  - **Avoid false proxies.** "If you run an ad and you're keeping track of clicks, you're making a mistake."
  - **Genre.** "Genre does not mean generic, genre means what do I expect?" Does looking like everyone else
    "increase trust or does it get in the way of creating the tension people will need to go forward?"
  - **Tension, not stress.** Tension is "pulling the rubber band back". Real FOMO: "there's only 10,000 seats and
    we've already sold 9,000".
  - **"What will I tell my friends? What will I tell my spouse?"** "If you can't give them the story… they're not
    going to engage with you."
- "We're trying to win trust… connection… the benefit of the doubt. That doesn't happen because we're being
  authentic, it happens because we're being consistent, because we're creating tension, because we're building a
  bridge from here to there, because we're creating culture, people like us do things like this."
- Close: "who's it for, what's it for, what's worth doing, and who would miss us if we were gone."

**So for Project 1%**

- Richard's current axes are "money vs everything else". Most "have it all" programs already sit there (Wake Up
  Warrior has owned "married businessmen who want to have it all" since 2012). Don't play that game; pick axes
  where Richard can win. See the map in `02-positioning-and-offer.md`.
- "First 100 men, then the price goes up" is stress, not tension. Nobody wants to help Richard reach 100. A cohort
  that starts together on a real date is real tension: miss it and you start alone, later.
- The page currently copies the guru genre (blinking "Watch now", blood-red urgency, value stacks, price
  drop-downs). That pattern is what this buyer has learned to distrust.
- The spouse test is the big one for this persona: "I bought a $9 thing off a Facebook ad" is not a story. "I'm
  doing a 35-day training with a group of guys like me, starting Monday" is.

## The five questions to answer before any ad gets made

These come straight from the modules, and the other two documents answer them:

1. **Who's it for?** Name the smallest viable audience. See `03-primary-persona.md`.
2. **What's it for?** What change do we seek to make in that man?
3. **What's the promise, and where are we breaking it today?**
4. **What will he tell his wife and his friends?**
5. **What are we measuring instead of clicks?**
