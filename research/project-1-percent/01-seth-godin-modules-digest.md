# Seth Godin modules 2–6: what they actually teach, applied to Richard Norris

Source: the five uploaded transcripts (Module 2 to Module 6), re-read line by line on 10 Oct 2026. Quotes are
verbatim. "Applied" sections are the application to Richard and Project 1%.

This version replaces the first digest, which misread several ideas. The corrections are listed at the end.

---

## The one idea that runs through all five modules

The modules aren't five tips. They're one argument:

1. **Direct response is a drug.** It works, then decays, and while you're hooked on it you stop doing the real
   work (Module 2).
2. **The real work is building a brand**, which is **a promise people can predict and trust**, and which earns the
   benefit of the doubt (Module 3).
3. **You build it with a small, connected group** that shares a worldview and a story, not with "everyone"
   (Module 4).
4. **You keep it by being consistent**, not by being "authentic", and by making real decisions (Module 5).
5. **You claim it with a position**: a useful (not "best") place on a map where both ends are good. You choose your
   genre on purpose, create real tension, and hand people a story they can tell (Module 6).

In Seth's closing words: we're trying to win "trust… connection… the benefit of the doubt. That doesn't happen
because we're being authentic, it happens because we're being consistent, because we're creating tension, because
we're building a bridge from here to there, because we're creating culture, people like us do things like this."

**Consistency, tension, a bridge, culture.** That's the brief for Richard.

## Module 2: brand advertising vs direct response

**The ideas**

- Direct marketing is measured action: spend $1, make $2, repeat "until you make all the dollars that it's possible
  to make, and then you should stop." It's honest and focused, and it "doesn't scale": run a coupon too often or
  interrupt too many people "and it stops working."
- Meta is built to hook you: early results "will be great. And then inevitably, over time, it will get worse and
  worse."
- The hidden cost: hooked advertisers "stop paying attention to what matters, which is finding the tribe, earning
  the attention of the tribe, building conversations within and among the tribe, figure out how to matter to
  people as opposed to simply strip mining their attention."
- Permission ("anticipated, personal, and relevant messages to the people who want to get them") always pays for
  itself.
- The question: "Why am I even spending the time or the money? Am I measuring the right thing?"

**Applied to Richard**

- The current launch is pure strip-mining: 13 cold ads into a 25-minute sales video with a $9 checkout. It may work
  for a while. It builds nothing.
- Richard already has what most launches don't: permission. Years of property clients, YouTube subscribers, email
  lists, and people who paid him and trust him. The brand gets built there first.
- Ads still matter, but as amplifiers of a brand that already stands for something, not as the brand.

## Module 3: culture and stories worth sharing

**The ideas**

- **"A brand is a promise," not a logo.** The test: if Nike opened a hotel, you'd know what it would be like. If
  Hyatt made sneakers, you'd have no idea. "Nike has a brand, but Hyatt just has a logo."
- **Brand earns the benefit of the doubt.** People rated Bing's results higher when they carried the Google logo.
- **Two loyalty tests:** do people pay more for yours? When you announce something, do they care?
- **The strategist's questions:** "What kind of brand are you building? How long is it going to take? How much is
  it going to cost? Who do you want to build that brand with? Are you even measuring the right thing?" "If we
  disappeared, who would miss us? What is the change we seek to make?"
- **Rivian:** "Does an ad on Facebook ever sell a $96,000 pickup truck? I don't think so. But someone decided to
  make a pickup truck, not a sports car. That decision was strategic." The strategic choice of who it's for and what
  it is does the selling. Ads don't.
- **Psychographics over demographics.** Two people both own houses, but only one reads Martha Stewart. "What do
  people believe before we even get there? What do people want before we get there?… where are we finding our pain
  and our fear and our dreams?" Worldview decides what people believe, look for and pay for.
- **People want three things:** status ("who's up, who's down"), affiliation ("who's like me"), and freedom from fear.
- **TOMS:** $80 shoes with a logo, sold first to "almost no one", which was "exactly the right thing to do": a tiny
  group of women who wanted to be first and feel like philanthropists. "Nice shoes, where'd you get them?" gave the
  wearer status and gave the friend tension ("she's behind"). Repeat until it sold for half a billion. TOMS coffee
  failed because nobody asks "interesting coffee, where'd you get it?" No conversation, no tribe. It became "here's
  the social media ad, please buy some coffee."
- **The real difference:** "I'm going to help this group of people get to where they want to go."

**Applied to Richard**

- **The Nike-hotel test for Richard:** if "Richard Norris" opened a gym, wrote a book or hosted a retreat, would we
  know what it would feel like? Today, no. Project 1% is a logo on a guru-style funnel. The aim is a brand
  where the answer is obvious (see the repositioning document).
- **Rivian applied:** no ad will fix Project 1%. The decision about who it's for and what it is will.
- **The persona must start from worldview.** Not "men 35–50 earning $X", but what they already believe (see the
  persona document).
- **Project 1% needs a "nice shoes" moment:** something a member has, says or does that makes another man ask "what's
  that?" Today there's nothing. Nobody asks about a $9 course.

## Module 4: the smallest viable audience

**The ideas**

- The agency's boy-or-girl debate. Seth's "make it neither" compromise was the idiot answer, because averaging to
  please everyone kills it. The goal is "not the biggest possible audience, but the smallest viable audience."
- **Put a number on it:** "How few people would you need to delight or thrill? How few people would need to benefit
  from your network effect for your project to work?" Picking the number "puts you on the hook."
- **The Newton** sold 100,000 units at $1,000 in its first month, $100M, and still failed. "They weren't a group.
  They were just a bunch. They weren't connected. They weren't in it for the same reason. They weren't responding
  to the same story."
- **The ratchet:** give them "something that they want to talk about. Their group of people wants to hear. And then…
  a ratchet where more connection and more action leads to more status and more affiliation."
- **Purple Cow in a milk carton:**
  - Seth went first to the people who already gave him the benefit of the doubt (his readers), for $5.
  - He sold 12-packs, so buyers had to give copies away, which made them look like generous leaders.
  - The carton sat on desks and "sent a message to your boss… let's talk about this." Readers spread it because if
    their boss bought into the idea, *their own* work lives got better.
  - Result: more than a million copies.

**Applied to Richard**

- The funnel speaks to a bunch, not a group: the 50-year-old rich and empty man, "24 or 54, broke or doing well",
  "men chasing more". Pick one group that responds to one story.
- **Put Richard on the hook with a number.** For example: 100 founding men, then 1,000 finishers in year one who'd
  miss Project 1% if it disappeared.
- **Start where he has permission** (his property clients and audience), the way Seth started with his readers.
- **Design the 12-pack and the milk carton:**
  - A reason to bring another man in (a brother, a business partner, a best mate), because *his* life gets better
    if that man changes too.
  - An object that sits in the member's world and says "let's talk about this" to his partner.

## Module 5: building an authentic voice

**The ideas**

- **"Authenticity is a crock… authenticity is for friends and family."** With everyone else, "professionals don't
  bring authenticity. They bring consistency."
- **The musician with a headache still brings the show. The lawyer stays the lawyer they promised to be.** "Your brand
  is a promise. Are you gonna keep it or not?… make a promise and keep it."
- **Agencies break consistency** when they're rewarded for awards or clicks: "one offs… ads that don't match the
  promise of the brand… gimmicks." A purple cow isn't a gimmick; it's a service: "this is who we are and what we
  believe in. This is the change we're here to make. Do you want to come on this journey or not?"
- **Algorithms reward going off script and picking fights** "'cause that makes the social media companies more money.
  Not 'cause it makes you succeed."
- **"We don't make ads… We make a difference. We make choices. We make decisions.** And that's hard to do. We've been
  indoctrinated not to show up to make decisions."
- **Good decisions are not good outcomes (Annie Duke).** A good creative decision means you don't run out of time or
  money, and you're "strategically coherent": you've thought hard about the change, who it's for and what it's for.
  If it still fails, "we've learned one thing that doesn't work."

**Applied to Richard**

- Richard's stories are true, and you lived them alongside him. So the issue isn't whether he's authentic. It's
  whether every touchpoint keeps **one promise**, with one story, one set of numbers and one framework. Ads, video,
  page, checkout, lessons, community, emails, the call.
- "If you're a simp, don't click" and "this might make you angry" are algorithm fights. They get reach and spend
  the brand.
- This work has to end in **decisions**: one persona, one position, one promise. Then tests run as good decisions
  that teach something either way.

## Module 6: ad positioning (the core module)

### 1. "Positioning is a generous act."

Seth's opening line, repeated at the end, frames everything. Positioning isn't a weapon against competitors. It's a
service to customers: it helps the right person recognise "this is for me", and the wrong person go somewhere better.

### 2. The XY grid, done honestly

- Draw two axes, find "a square, a corner, a quadrant that's under-inhabited, and then filling it."
- The honesty rule: choose axes "where either axis is a good thing." Cheap vs expensive, convenient vs hard: each
  end is right for somebody.
- **The implication:** the competitor at the other end isn't wrong or bad. They're right for different people. You
  don't win by trashing them. You win by being clearly right for your people.

### 3. Volvo and Ferrari

- If someone walks into Volvo to "win a big car race", Volvo should send him to Ferrari. If someone walks into
  Ferrari with seven kids and a preschool run, Ferrari should send him to Volvo.
- **"What it means to have a position is that you can eagerly and honestly send people to what others think of as
  your competition."**
- **Test for Richard:** can he name, out loud and warmly, who he'd send elsewhere, and where? If not, there's no
  position yet.

### 4. Useful, not best

- **"There isn't a best position, there's simply a useful position."**
- Useful to whom? To the smallest viable audience. It must help them choose. And "you need to claim it": say it out
  loud and be on the hook.

### 5. Supreme and life insurance

- **Supreme** doesn't say "you need a t-shirt, we have a t-shirt." Its position is "for people who view clothing as a
  sign of status and a certain kind of affiliation… please, don't come in here and tell us your stuff's too
  expensive, it's not for you." Price is part of the position. The position is about what buying it *means*.
- **Life insurance** that's "just like their life insurance" has no position, "you might even have a slogan… but you
  don't have a position." Slogans aren't positions.
- **"Stand for something and not decide to stand for everything."**

### 6. The strategy cards

**a. Don't play games you can't win.**

- "You can't out Amazon Amazon, you can't out Walmart Walmart, you can't out Apple Apple. These slots… are taken, but
  there are lots of things you can do that Amazon can't… go find one of those things and claim it."
- This is about **giants**: the dominant players whose game is scale, fame and resources. It is not about avoiding
  a peer who serves a similar audience.
- For Richard, the giants are the household names of men's self-improvement: Hormozi (business scale), Huberman and
  Attia (scientific and medical authority), Goggins (extreme toughness), Tony Robbins (events), Bartlett (media).
  Richard can't out-Hormozi Hormozi or out-Huberman Huberman.
- **The real question is what Richard can do that they can't.** Answered in the repositioning document.

**b. Avoid false proxies.**

- "Easy to measure and distracting": a president's height, a programmer's typing speed, hiring Ivy League tennis
  captains. "If you run an ad and you're keeping track of clicks, you're making a mistake."
- **This card is Richard's whole message.** His story is a man who measured himself by a false proxy (net worth,
  then his physique) while the things that mattered went unmeasured: "a scorecard that only had one line on it." The
  program is an anti-false-proxy system. The marketing should practise what the product preaches, and not
  optimise for clicks or $9 sales.

**c. Every genre is a category. "Genre does not mean generic, genre means what do I expect? What does this remind me of?"**

- People need to file you somewhere ("a mystery novel needs to feel like a mystery novel").
- But "tweets look like tweets and Instagram posts look like Instagram posts, but why? Who decided that? Is it
  helping us or not?… Does matching the way our website looks compared to their website increase trust or does it
  get in the way of creating the tension people will need to go forward?"
- **Genre is a choice, and it's part of positioning.** Project 1% currently picked the "online guru VSL funnel" genre,
  which sets the expectation of a cheap course and hype. Choosing a different genre (a cohort, a members' circle, a
  documented rebuild) changes what people expect, what they'll pay, and whether they trust it.
- **At the ad level,** this is also why native ads work. A static that looks like a real post from a real man, or a
  UGC clip from a member, fits the feed's genre and earns attention as a person, not as an ad.

**d. Tension (not stress).**

- Stress is being pulled two ways at once. "Tension is the hard work we do of pulling the rubber band back before we
  let it go."
- Real FOMO: "there's only 10,000 seats and we've already sold 9,000."
- **"Just the fact that we showed up creates tension."** A new Mac exists, and now "how will I feel that I don't
  have the newest one anymore?"
- **Applied:** tension should come from the culture existing ("men like me are doing this and I'm not"), and from real
  constraints (a cohort that starts on a date, real seats), not from "first 100" countdowns.

**e. "What will I tell my friends? What will I tell my boss? What will I tell my spouse?"**

- Without a story you give them, the only story left is "I did what we did last time, or I got the same thing but
  cheaper."
- With network effects, people feel the tension and ask this question. "If you can't give them the story or help
  them construct a story that works for them, they're not going to engage with you."
- **Applied:** at $9, the only story is "it was only nine quid", which is exactly Seth's "same thing but cheaper"
  fallback. We have to design the story he tells his partner and his mates.

### 7. The close

- **Be on the hook:** "raise your hand and be on the hook, to make your assertions out loud, to determine what you're
  going to measure and what you're going to intentionally ignore."
- **Choosing where to show up means choosing where not to.** "Can you announce what those choices are and why, and can
  you be a professional and lean in to what you stand for?"
- **The tribe:** "the smallest viable audience… members of a tribe… people who seek more connection, who want to stand
  with each other and for something, and who want to go somewhere."
- **The work:** "we don't get to skip all these things and just say to the client, what kind of ad do you want… We're
  not trying to win awards, we're trying to win trust."
- **"Who's it for, what's it for, what's worth doing, and who would miss us if we were gone."**
- **It's earned, not bought:** "you can't buy your way out of this problem… It's incremental, it grows over time."
- **"Go make a ruckus":** "find a place where you would be missed if you didn't show up."
