The short answer

The question behind this worksheet: were the 13 live Project 1% ads built from a psychology plan, or made first and planned later? Each section answers one part of the plan, sets what it should be, and checks the live ads against it.

Verdict
Strong instincts and a strong founder story, but no plan connecting them. Every ad tries to do the whole journey at once, so no ad does any one job well.

What the evidence shows

  1. Every ad targets the same awareness level, then runs the whole funnel. 10 of 13 hooks speak to men who already feel the problem. Then each ad races through credentials, mechanism, offer and guarantee. No ad is built to reframe the problem, prove the method, or close the sale.
  2. The test can't tell you which message works. All 13 ads share the same headline and the same 600-word caption. Only the video changes.
  3. The most-delivered ad is the full 25-minute sales video, shown to cold audiences. That's a closing asset used as an opener.
  4. The emotional driver is off-target. The caption opens on a "$200,000 Porsche 911 Turbo" and closes on "the Rolex". It sells status objects to men whose pain is that status objects didn't work. The drivers the research points to (protecting loved ones, freedom from pain and fear, belonging) are mentioned once or twice and never lead.
  5. Some persuasion principles are breaking trust. Scarcity changes at every step. The price anchor jumps from "under $97" to a "$5,626 value" for $9. A free gift is promised in the video and never delivered.

What's already working (keep it)

  • Richard's true story is the strongest asset in the account: the slipped discs, the floor of the apartment in 2025, the relationship he lost. It's the "pratfall effect" (see section 3) waiting to lead.
  • Strong individual hooks: "Most men upgrade the car, the income, the title. Never the operating system" (ad #6), and "This is specifically for men already winning on paper" (ads #4 and #10).
  • Ad #9, "Stop fixing one pillar", is the closest to on-strategy: honest, vulnerable, and no confusing price line.
  • Format variety is already there: talking head, kinetic text, whiteboard, motion graphics.

SECTION 1

Audience Awareness Assessment

Where does the target man sit on Eugene Schwartz's awareness spectrum? The target is the self-made builder of 32–48 who has compounded his money and depreciated himself.

Unaware Small share

Doesn't know he has a problem.

A few men still read their exhaustion as "just a busy season". They're reached by pattern interrupts, not pitches.

Problem aware Primary

Knows the problem, not the solution.

Most of the market. He feels the symptoms: burnout, fog, emptiness, absence at home, the weight of every decision alone.

Solution aware Secondary

Knows solutions exist, not this one.

He has already tried the usual fixes: hire more people, take a break, sell the business, read The E-Myth, a 75-day challenge, therapy, testosterone.

Product aware Secondary

Knows the product, hasn't bought.

Richard's warm audience (property clients, YouTube, email) knows Richard but not Project 1%. It's small, but it trusts him most.

Most aware Retargeting only

Ready to buy with the right offer.

Men who watched the video, visited the page or started checkout. This is where the $9 bet belongs.

Market sophistication: high

"Balance", "have it all", "1%", "brotherhood" and "challenge" are claims he has heard many times. Schwartz's answer for a market like this: reframe the problem and show a unique mechanism.

What the research shows

He's aware of the symptoms but misdiagnoses the cause. He treats it as an operations problem (hire, delegate, sell, take a break) or a willpower problem (grind harder, another challenge).

"Should I sell and move on or get fat off the profit and hate life??"Business owner, 20+ years in the industry, Florida
"I feel like this might just be an escape fantasy."Manufacturing owner
"It's not the hours I work, but that I can't be present with my family when I'm not working."Entrepreneur, 35, married with a baby
"Hours were never my problem. The thing that wrecked me was carrying every open decision in my head all weekend."Founder replying in a thread

Based on this assessment, the primary messaging approach should be:

Validate, reframe, then show the mechanism. Name his symptoms in his own words so he feels seen ("it's not just me"). Then reframe the cause: it isn't the hours, the staff or the business model. He has built systems for everything except the man running it ("compounding money, depreciating yourself"). Next, show the mechanism (ten rules written on day 1, one lesson a day, a nine-line scorecard). Show the offer (the $9 bet) only to men who have already seen the reframe.
Current adsAll 13 ads open at problem aware and close at most aware within the same ad. No ad is dedicated to the reframe (for the solution-aware man) or to the offer (for retargeting). The ad Meta delivers most, the full 25-minute sales video, is a most-aware asset served to cold, problem-aware audiences.

Emotional Drivers Analysis

Which core human needs does Project 1% address? Selections are based on what these men say in public forums, not on what the ads currently say.

Maslow's Hierarchy

  • Physiological (basic survival needs)Pain and sleep show up, but they aren't why he buys.
  • Safety (security, stability, protection) SecondaryProtecting what he built: his body, his family, "losing half the business in a divorce".
  • Belonging (connection, relationships, community) Primary"Every other man I know has a 9–5, so there's nobody I can really ask." Presence at home. A brotherhood.
  • Esteem (recognition, status, respect, achievement) PrimarySelf-respect and respect at home: "I'm always the problem solver, not the problem bringer."
  • Self-actualization (growth, creativity, purpose) SecondaryThe lost drive: "that drive/spark is gone", "I'm in a fog". Who he becomes next.

Life-Force 8 Drives

  • Survival, enjoyment of life, life extension SecondaryA body that still works at 60. Actually enjoying what he paid for.
  • Enjoyment of food and beverages
  • Freedom from fear, pain and danger PrimaryThe back, the burnout, the marriage going quiet, the fear of losing it all.
  • Sexual companionshipPresent underneath (desirability, testosterone), but never lead with it.
  • Comfortable living conditionsHe already has them. That's the point.
  • To be superior; winning SecondaryHe still wants to win. Reframe it as winning without paying the hidden bill.
  • Care and protection of loved ones Primary"Our baby is never going to be a baby again." Present for his wife and kids.
  • Social approval SecondaryThe respect of his wife, his kids and his peers. The man they copy.

The primary emotional transformation Project 1% delivers:

FromSuccessful on paper, empty and alone with it. Never off. Never fully home. Carrying every decision himself while his body sends the bill.
→
ToStill building, but whole. Present at home, strong in his body, clear in his head, and no longer alone with it.
Current adsThe lead driver today is status and comfort objects: the Porsche, the Rolex, the penthouse, "the 1%", "an elite body". That pulls on To be superior and Comfortable living, which this man already has. Care of loved ones appears as one line ("make it home for dinner"). Belonging is a phrase ("a private brotherhood") that's never shown. Richard's injuries are used as credentials, not as the viewer's own fear.

SECTION 3

Persuasion Principles Selection

Pick 2–3 primary principles to lead the creative, and use the rest only in support. The current ads use seven or more in a single ad.

Pratfall effect Primary

Strategic vulnerability for credibility

How: lead with the scars before the wins. The discs at 31, the floor in 2025, the stepkids' games he went to without really being there. "I was the coach with the penthouse and no health."

Today: used, but undercut by the Porsche, Rolex and St Regis lines.

Unity Primary

Creating tribal identity

How: "builders with something to lose": men who solve every problem except their own. A cohort that starts together, and a brotherhood earned by finishing.

Today: weak. "If you're a simp, don't click" pushes men out instead of bringing the right ones in.

Commitment Primary

Small yeses leading to bigger ones

How: the $9 bet → write your ten rules on day 1 → 35 lessons → the finishers' brotherhood → the strategy call.

Today: the "I give my word" contract is at checkout (good), but the call is pushed before day 1, which breaks the ladder.

Creative Evaluation Checklist

The 10-point check, applied to the 13 live ads as a set. ✓ passes · ~ partly · ✗ fails.

CheckTodayWhy
Does it clearly address the right awareness level?~The hooks are problem aware, but each ad then runs to the offer. Ad #1 (a bottom-funnel asset) is served cold.
Does it tap into at least one powerful emotional driver?✓Yes, but the lead driver is status objects, not the research drivers (loved ones, fear and pain, belonging).
Does it emphasize transformation, not just features?~#3, #9 and #11 sell transformation. #4, #6 and #10 drift into features (four pillars, 35 lessons, a nine-line scorecard).
Does it leverage 2–3 persuasion principles effectively?✗Seven or more are stacked per ad. Scarcity and anchoring are inconsistent, and the promised gift is never delivered.
Is the hook strong enough to stop the scroll?~"Most men upgrade the car…" and "winning on paper" are strong. "Dumb idea" is used four times. "Might make you angry" is guru genre.
Is the primary benefit immediately clear?~"Build a 1% life" is abstract. "Make it home for dinner" is clear but arrives at second 12.
Would I be excited to see it as the target customer?~Richard's to-camera stories, yes. The Porsche and Rolex lines and "simp", no.
Have I created pattern interrupts or distinctive elements?✓Good format variety: kinetic text, whiteboard, operating-system graphics, two-camera talking head.
Does it address objections the audience might have?~The guarantee is in every ad. "I'm too busy", "another guru" and "I've tried challenges" are only handled in the video's closing FAQ.
Is the call to action clear and compelling?✗The ad price differs from checkout. The "button below this video" doesn't exist in the feed. The "short video" is 25 minutes. The "page that breaks down all 35 lessons" doesn't.

Areas for improvement

  1. One price, one promise, from ad to checkout. The $9 bet, with a completion guarantee.
  2. Split the creative by awareness stage (section 6) instead of every ad doing everything.
  3. Rewrite the shared caption. Drop the Porsche and Rolex. Lead with loved ones, freedom from pain, belonging.
  4. Lead with vulnerability, then authority. Scars first, credentials second.
  5. Remove fake scarcity and inflated value. Deliver the promised free gift.
  6. Give each ad its own headline and caption, so tests measure the message, not just the video.
  7. Move the 25-minute video to retargeting. Build 30–60 second entry ads for cold audiences.
  8. Make the page deliver what the ads promise: curriculum, price, guarantee.

Winning Creative Formulas

Four proven structures, each written out for Project 1% from Richard's true story and the men's own words.

Problem → Agitation → Solution
Problem
"You're never actually off. Even on holiday, the business is in your head."
Agitate
"Your kids get what's left. Your back sends the bill. And the only person you'd call about it is you."
Solution
"35 days. One lesson a day. Ten rules you write on day 1, so the decisions are made before the 6am argument starts."
Feature → Advantage → Benefit
Feature
One lesson a day, most of them shorter than your commute.
Advantage
It fits a calendar that's already full. Nothing to add, nothing to reschedule.
Benefit
You change without stepping away from the business you built.
Before → After → Bridge
Before
"Early 2025: business at its best, me on the floor of my apartment, no one to call."
After
"Training again. Back intact. In the room when it matters."
Bridge
"The 35 principles I rebuilt on. That's Project 1%."
Objection → Counter → Reframe
Objection
"I've tried challenges. They last six weeks."
Counter
"This isn't a test of willpower. It runs on rules you wrote while your head was clear."
Reframe
"You don't need more discipline. You need fewer decisions."
Remember

Psychology is how we find the right man and say something true in his own words. It is never a license to invent scarcity, inflate value or promise what the program can't deliver. For a brand whose promise is honesty, that line is the strategy.

Next steps

  1. Fact sheet: agree one version of every number in Richard's story, used everywhere.
  2. Fix the funnel (price, page, gift, call disclosure) before increasing spend.
  3. Brief the first batch of creative by awareness stage: native statics, Richard to camera, the transformation, then member UGC after cohort 1.
  4. Judge the tests on finish rate, refunds and qualified calls, not cost per $9 sale alone.
